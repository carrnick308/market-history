These results leave out fees and tax, which can differ between the two. Drip-feeding can mean more transaction fees (several purchases instead of one), and interest on money waiting to go in is taxed. Both usually tilt things a little further toward the lump sum, but it depends on your platform and tax position.

Lump sum has usually come out ahead because markets have risen more often than they've fallen, so money waiting on the sidelines tends to miss out. Drip-feeding usually costs a little, but it limits how bad you'd feel if the market fell straight after you invested. If spreading it out is what gets you to invest at all, that's worth more than the small difference shown here.

About these numbers

  • Data: Robert J. Shiller, U.S. Stock Markets 1871–Present and CAPE Ratio (shillerdata.com). US share market (S&P 500) with dividends reinvested. No fees or tax. Prices are monthly averages.
  • Start dates run from January 1934, when the US Treasury bill rate (what waiting money earns here) begins. In NZ dollars they start in March 1985, when the dollar was floated.
  • An all-time high is a month when the US index (before inflation) set a new record. A high P/E is Shiller’s cyclically adjusted P/E above 30 (see the note under the first table for why 30). After a 20% fall is a month when the index was at least 20% below its previous high.
  • The groups overlap, and neighbouring start months share most of their history, so treat the percentages as a guide rather than precise odds. Groups marked “few” rest on little history.
  • NZ dollar results convert at each month’s RBNZ exchange rate, unhedged. Waiting money is assumed to earn the US Treasury bill rate in both currencies, which is close to NZ rates over long periods but not month to month.
  • Looking for something broader? Try the market timing calculator or the Shiller P/E calculator.
  • This is general information about past market behaviour, not personalised financial advice. Past returns don’t tell you future returns.
